# Philadelphia — Founding ideas and the people who widened them

A city of public promises and persistent questions. Follow the Declaration, Ona Judge, Mother Bethel and the making of national memory through eight connected readings.

## Before the founding story

Philadelphia’s national story is easier to understand when it is placed inside a longer history of people and land. Lenape communities lived in the Delaware Valley before the colonial city became a setting for independence. The Delaware Tribe of Indians explains the relationship between the names Lenape and Delaware and describes its continuing work in government, language and culture. These are living people, not simply the opening scene of someone else’s story.

A historical study hosted by the Tribe examines Indigenous villages in and around Philadelphia through early records. Such records need interpretation: a colonial map is evidence of what its maker recorded, not a complete account of what mattered to the people who lived there.

Let that distinction guide this ORB. Buildings, documents and anniversaries make some things visible while leaving others outside the frame. Beginning with place makes room for the Declaration, a church and a woman’s escape without allowing any one landmark to stand for everyone.

## A declaration in a room

On July 4, 1776, the Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence. The familiar engrossed parchment was signed beginning on August 2; adoption and signing were related events, not one single moment. A document that seems fixed in a display case emerged through drafting, debate, printing and circulation.

Its political argument joined a claim about human equality to a statement that the colonies would be independent states. That combination helps explain its enduring importance: it announced a particular break with Britain while using language that later readers could apply more widely. Reading its words is different from assuming everyone in 1776 enjoyed the freedom they described.

For this collection, the useful question is what people did with a promise once it existed in public. Follow the Constitution to examine institutional design, or Ona Judge to encounter freedom as a decision made under danger. The distance between an ideal and a life is part of the history.

- [Declaration of Independence (1776)](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/declaration-of-independence) — National Archives

## Designing a government

Declaring independence did not, by itself, specify how a national government would make laws, execute them or judge disputes. The Constitution signed in 1787 addressed a different task: assigning powers and creating institutions. Its first three articles establish legislative, executive and judicial branches. A reader can follow those divisions in the document itself rather than treating “the founders” as a single voice.

Institutional design also records limits and compromises. The original text included provisions protecting slavery’s interests, even without using the word slavery. A structure can distribute authority while still excluding people from its benefits. Later amendments are therefore part of the constitutional story, not merely footnotes to a finished achievement.

Try reading one provision as a practical arrangement: who receives power, who checks its exercise, and whose position is left unresolved? Philadelphia offers both an inspiring act of political invention and a document that invites close examination of what was built into it.

- [Constitution of the United States (1787)](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/constitution) — National Archives

## Freedom, seen from one life

In May 1796, Ona Judge escaped enslavement at the President’s House in Philadelphia. She had served in George and Martha Washington’s household. The National Park Service traces her departure and subsequent life in New Hampshire, including the continuing threat posed by attempts to return her to slavery.

Her story changes the scale of the city’s founding narrative. Here, freedom is not a word on a public document or a quality attributed to an entire nation. It is something a person pursued, with consequences for her work, family and safety. Her survival and choices belong at the center of the account; the household she left supplies context rather than the sole reason she matters.

This reading does not depend on the contents of any present-day exhibition panel. It points to a documented life. Set it beside the Declaration and ask a precise question: what does the language of liberty look like when read from the position of someone denied it?

- [Ona Judge Escapes to Freedom](https://home.nps.gov/articles/independence-oneyjudge.htm) — National Park Service

## Building institutions of your own

Mother Bethel’s own history describes the conversion of a blacksmith’s shop into a place of worship and its dedication in 1794. The congregation’s successive buildings tell a story of institution-building: a community making durable space for religious life and collective purpose. Richard Allen’s role matters, but the congregation is more than the biography of one founder.

Sarah Allen widens the view. The National Park Service’s account connects her with care during Philadelphia’s yellow-fever crisis and with antislavery work. Attending to that work helps recover the households, relationships and practical support behind a public institution.

A useful way to read this history is to ask what people could accomplish together that they could not accomplish alone. A building provided a meeting place; sustained organization provided continuity. The city’s contribution to American civic life includes people creating their own institutions when existing ones denied them equal standing. Follow the sources to keep those contributions specific.

## A nation stages its hundredth birthday

Philadelphia hosted the Centennial Exposition in 1876, one hundred years after the Declaration. The Library of Congress preserves photographs and other visual material from the exhibition. These offer an opportunity to examine how a country presents itself: what it displays, how it arranges a visitor’s attention, and what it treats as an achievement.

Choose one image from the collection and begin with what is actually visible. Is it a building, an exhibit, a crowd or a carefully arranged view? A photograph can document an object while also participating in the celebration around it. Its date, caption and maker help determine what it can support.

The interpretive question is larger than whether the exhibition looked impressive. Which version of the nation did it make easy to see? Which experiences would require a different source? An anniversary is itself a historical object. That idea will reappear in Detroit, where Rivera’s murals make work visible through an artist’s choices.

- [Centennial Exposition, 1876](https://www.loc.gov/free-to-use/centennial-exposition-1876/) — Library of Congress

## Which parts of a landmark are old?

Independence Hall is a historic building with a history of alteration and restoration. The National Park Service’s architectural account describes changes made after the founding period, including a nineteenth-century replacement of the tower and later efforts to recover earlier appearances. The building encountered by a visitor is not an untouched room preserved outside time.

That does not make it less valuable. It makes preservation another story to read. A restorer must decide which period to represent, which physical evidence to trust and how to handle missing fabric. An archival picture, a reconstructed feature and surviving material answer different questions.

Look at a dated image beside a present-day view and identify one feature whose history you can verify. This small exercise makes a large principle tangible: public memory is maintained through decisions. The people who preserve a landmark help shape how later generations encounter it, just as the people who once worked inside shaped its first meanings.

- [Independence Hall: Architecture](https://www.nps.gov/inde/learn/historyculture/places-independencehall-architecture.htm) — National Park Service

## What should a 250th birthday remember?

The 250-year anniversary invites a choice about attention. One possible celebration centers the achievement of independence. Another follows the ways people tried to widen the freedom it promised. The readings in this ORB suggest that these approaches can be held together through specific evidence rather than an uncomplicated verdict about the past.

You have encountered a national document, a government’s design, Ona Judge’s decision, a community institution and the later preservation of a founding landmark. They are different kinds of historical evidence. No single one can do the work of all the others.

Here is an editorial invitation, not a claim of public consensus: choose one person, object or place that deserves a larger role in the next telling of Philadelphia’s story. Explain what your source shows and what it leaves uncertain. A thoughtful celebration can express gratitude for what people made while remaining curious about who is still missing from the picture.
